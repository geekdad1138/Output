MIT just made a breakthrough to make LiDAR devices over 1,000 times smaller and more reliable.

Common LiDAR devices for autonomous vehicles can be 5-8 inches, weigh 2-5 pounds, and have lots of moving parts.

The scientists at MIT figured out a way to take those mechanical parts and put them all on a tiny chip only a few millimeters wide and weighing less than one gram.

Usually LiDAR has problems being that small because the lasers interfere with each other at close range, called “crosstalk”.

The solution? Geometry!
By changing the shape of the antennas they could put them closer together and then use SCIENCE to detect the different signals from each laser.

From the MIT article:
“MIT researchers designed a set of three antennas with different geometries, varying the width of each antenna and the size and arrangement of corrugations. With varied geometries, each antenna has a different propagation coefficient, which determines how light travels down the antenna.”

The magnitude of this change is hard to overstate. 
We’re talking about a device volume change of 1,000 to 10,000 times smaller, and with no moving parts.

Instead of a spinning coffee can sized mechanical unit on top of a car, we can embed a postage stamp sized chip in a headlight assembly.

It can be embedded in the tip of a robotic arm, on the wing of a drone, inside a mobile phone, or even on a wearable set of augmented reality glasses.

The sci-fi come true research was done by MIT’s Jelena Notaros, Henry Crawford-Eng, Andres Garcia Coleto, Benjamin Mazur, Daniel M. DeSantis, and Tal Sneh.
